# NVMe small-random-read techniques for the engram table — research report

## 0. Prediction, written before searching

*I expected io_uring with `O_DIRECT` into registered buffers to win, because it collapses 48 syscalls into one `io_uring_enter` and removes the page-fault path and the 16× read amplification at the same time.*

That prediction was **half right and mis-sized**. It is the right interface, but it is not the biggest lever, and the number I would have attached to it was too optimistic by 3–7×. See §8.

Two facts found during the round changed the ranking, one from the repository and one from the kernel source:

- The measurement in `docs/plan.md` never separated **caller CPU time** from **wall latency**. After the overlap arm, essentially all of the remaining 310 µs is CPU, and `AGENTS.md` already states the governing rule: "the calling thread's serial time is the step's length". Moving the 48 reads to a helper thread removes ~221 µs from the step on kernel 6.8 with **zero** new interfaces. That is rank 1 or 2 and no source was needed to find it.
- `IORING_OP_MADVISE` is unconditionally forced to io-wq on 6.8 (verified in the source). Batching the advise calls through io_uring would make things **worse**, not better.

## 1. Cost decomposition (derived from your own arms, before any technique)

Your table already contains enough to split the 310 µs into terms. All four lines below are **derived from your measured numbers**, not measured by me.

| term | value | how derived |
|---|---:|---|
| syscall crossing, per `madvise` | 0.83 µs | "resident rows still advised" arm: 40 µs ÷ 48 |
| in-kernel readahead work, per row | 3.77 µs | (221 − 40) ÷ 48 |
| minor fault, per row | 1.88 µs | 90 µs ÷ 48 |
| device wait, exposed | ≈ 0 µs | 310 − 221 − 90 ≈ 0 in the pipelined arm |

The consequence sets the whole ranking: **only 40 µs of the 221 µs submit cost is syscall crossing.** The other 181 µs is `do_madvise` → page allocation, page-cache insertion, extent lookup and bio construction, and *any* technique that still calls `do_madvise` per row pays it. Every batching interface (`process_madvise`, `IORING_OP_MADVISE`, `IORING_OP_FADVISE`) has a ceiling of 40 µs saved out of 311. That kills four of the spec's candidates on arithmetic alone.

Read amplification is also not where the time goes. 48 × 4 KiB = 192 KiB per token; at this drive's 7.4 GB/s sequential rating that is **26.6 µs of device transfer** (my estimate), against 13,056 B of payload that would cost 1.8 µs. Removing the 16× amplification buys ~25 µs of *device* time, which is already hidden. `O_DIRECT` is worth doing for what it removes from the **CPU** path, not for the bytes.

## 3. Per-item detail

### 3.1 io_uring on kernel 6.8 — what is actually there

All of the following are present on 6.8 and need no kernel change: registered (fixed) buffers and `READ_FIXED` (5.1), registered files (5.1), `IORING_SETUP_SINGLE_ISSUER` (6.0), `IORING_SETUP_DEFER_TASKRUN` (6.1), `IORING_OP_URING_CMD` NVMe passthrough (5.19). Source: [io_uring_setup(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/io_uring_setup.2.html) and [the setup-flags overview](https://manpages.ubuntu.com/manpages/resolute/man7/io_uring_setup_flags.7.html). `DEFER_TASKRUN` + `SINGLE_ISSUER` is the documented recommended default for a single-threaded ring with its own event loop, and it is what you want: completion work runs only inside your own `io_uring_enter`, which is exactly the prefetch-helper shape.

**The CPU saving is smaller than it looks, and two good sources disagree on it.** Haas & Leis give a total budget of 13,000 cycles per I/O at 12.5 M IOPS on a 64-core 2.5 GHz machine and state that "around half of the available CPU cores are consumed by the OS just for submitting and reaping I/O requests" — roughly 6,500 cycles ≈ 2.6 µs per I/O for interrupt-mode io_uring, and that was measured with **the filesystem, RAID and the page cache disabled** ([PVLDB 16(9):2090, 2023](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p2090-haas.pdf)). The 2026 DBMS paper reports that "modest batch sizes (e.g., 16 operations) reduce the CPU cycles per operation by roughly 5–6×", but its Table 1 shows batch read at 10,200 cycles against single read at 8,264, so those are per-**transaction** figures and cannot be divided by the batching factor ([arXiv 2512.04859](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04859v1), kernel 6.15, 8× Kioxia CM7-R). Taking both: **0.4–3 µs of caller CPU per I/O**, so 48 reads cost **19–144 µs** (my estimate from those two sources). Against your 311 µs, that is a saving of 170–290 µs, not the near-total elimination I predicted.

Feature deltas from the same paper, on YCSB throughput, each additive: registered buffers +11 % (238 k tx/s), passthrough +20 % (300 k), IOPOLL +21 % (376 k), SQPOLL +32 % (546 k). Treat these as throughput-at-saturation numbers, not latency numbers for a 48-op burst once per 10–40 ms.

**`IORING_OP_MADVISE` is an evidenced negative on 6.8.** From [`io_uring/advise.c` at tag v6.8](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/v6.8/io_uring/advise.c):

```c
int io_madvise_prep(struct io_kiocb *req, const struct io_uring_sqe *sqe)
{
	...
	req->flags |= REQ_F_FORCE_ASYNC;
	return 0;
```

`REQ_F_FORCE_ASYNC` is set unconditionally, so every `IORING_OP_MADVISE` is handed to an io-wq worker thread. `io_fadvise_prep` does the same for any advice other than `NORMAL`/`RANDOM`/`SEQUENTIAL`, so `FADV_WILLNEED` is also forced async. The 2026 DBMS paper measured io_worker handoff at **7.3 µs per operation** versus inline execution. 48 × 7.3 µs ≈ 350 µs — worse than the 221 µs you pay today with plain syscalls. This is the spec's item 1c, and the answer is a clean no.

**SQPOLL is also a negative for this workload.** The same paper measured that waking an idle SQPOLL thread "introduces a non-trivial latency of roughly 30 microseconds". Your ring is idle for the 10–40 ms between steps, so every token pays that wake. SQPOLL wins for continuously saturated rings; yours is the opposite shape.

**IOPOLL** requires both `O_DIRECT` and the boot-time `nvme.poll_queues=N` (default 0), per [io_uring_setup(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/io_uring_setup.2.html). It is a box configuration change that buys latency you have already hidden with a full step of lead time. Skip.

### 3.2 Direct I/O geometry — and a real contradiction

With 512 B logical and physical blocks, a 272 B row at an arbitrary offset spans 1 or 2 sectors. P(spans 2) = 271/512 = 0.529, so the mean read is 1.529 sectors = 783 B, giving **2.88× amplification** against 272 B of payload (my estimate; your measured mmap figure is 208,660 ÷ 13,056 = **15.98×**). Padding rows to 512 B would make it 1.88× but grows the table from 195 GiB to 367 GiB — not worth it.

**Contradiction between sources on sub-4K reads.** Haas & Leis measured the opposite of the spec's hope, on datacenter flash: "using smaller pages than 4 KB significantly decreases performance… 512 byte pages actually results in worse IOPS and latency on our SSDs… due to higher overhead in the flash translation layer, together with the fact that the flash hardware internally is not optimized for 512 byte pages" (Samsung PM1733, Fig. 3; latency minimum sits at 4 KB and rises below it — *read off the figure, approximate*). Against that, Micron demonstrated **230 million 512-byte random-read IOPS from a single server** (44 Micron 9650 Gen6 SSDs, 3 H100s, ≈118 GB/s, ~95 % of the drives' rated IOPS) at FMS 2026 ([StorageReview, Aug 2026](https://www.storagereview.com/news/nvidia-scada-puts-storage-control-on-the-gpu-as-cufile-goes-open-source)). The honest reading: sub-4K is not free on flash, it varies by controller generation, and on a 2023-era Phison Gen4 drive you should expect **512 B reads to cost about what 4 KB reads cost, not half**. Since amplification is worth only ~25 µs of hidden device time anyway (§1), issue 4 KB-aligned `O_DIRECT` reads if the 512 B path measures worse. Nothing in your budget depends on it.

### 3.3 Page-cache alternatives

- **`MADV_POPULATE_READ`** (5.14, on 6.8): "faulting in all pages in the range just as if manually reading from each page; however, avoid the actual memory access" ([madvise(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/madvise.2.html)). It folds your `WILLNEED`+touch into one call, removing the 48 minor faults (−90 µs) while keeping the 3.77 µs/row readahead cost. It is synchronous, so it only works on a helper thread. Cheapest new arm you can add.
- **`process_madvise`** (5.10): verified against [v6.8 `mm/madvise.c`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/v6.8/mm/madvise.c). `process_madvise_behavior_valid()` accepts exactly `{MADV_COLD, MADV_PAGEOUT, MADV_WILLNEED, MADV_COLLAPSE}` — so `MADV_WILLNEED` is allowed and `MADV_POPULATE_READ` is **not**. The body loops `do_madvise()` once per iovec entry, confirming the 40 µs ceiling. Crucially, **6.8 requires `CAP_SYS_NICE` unconditionally, including on yourself**; the self-exemption ("Since Linux 6.13, when manipulating the calling process, any advice flag is permitted") is [documented in process_madvise(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/process_madvise.2.html) and is not in your kernel. Also note `UIO_FASTIOV` is 8, so 48 ranges take a `kmalloc`.
- **`preadv2(RWF_NOWAIT)`**: returns `EAGAIN` rather than blocking. Useful as a *probe* — "is this row already cached?" — to decide whether to spend an I/O. Cheap and on 6.8.
- **`RWF_DONTCACHE`**: merged for **6.14**, reported at 65 % improvement with "no kswapd activity at all for reclaim" ([LWN 997548](https://lwn.net/Articles/997548/), [Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Uncached-Buffered-IO-Linux-6.14)). Not on 6.8. This is the single strongest argument for a kernel upgrade if you want to keep the buffered path.
- **Large folios on ext4**: still a patch series as of a v6 posting in September 2026, gated behind a `buffered_iomap` mount option and not default ([LWN 1020982](https://lwn.net/Articles/1020982/), [LWN 956575](https://lwn.net/Articles/956575/)). Even when it lands it is the wrong direction for you: a 64 KiB folio per 272 B row is worse amplification, not better.
- **`userfaultfd`**: keeps the fault, adds a userspace round trip. Strictly worse here.
- **`mlock` of a hot subset**: this is rank 2 in a different costume, and a plain `Vec` cache is simpler and does not fight reclaim.

### 3.4 GPU-initiated and GPU-direct storage

- **cuFile / GDS** works at 4 KiB granularity and is built for large contiguous transfers into GPU memory. Its poll mode does help small I/O — "reads of 4KB or less will poll for IO completion rather than blocking" ([GDS config guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/gpudirect-storage/configuration-guide/index.html)) — but your consumer wants 24 rows gathered into one contiguous 6,528 B vector per site. Gathering 48 scattered 272 B rows on the CPU into a pinned 13 KiB staging buffer and issuing one `cudaMemcpyAsync` is both simpler and cheaper than 48 GDS reads.
- **BaM** (ASPLOS'23) reported 5.3× over CPU-initiated access for analytics and up to 21.7× lower hardware cost for graph workloads from flash ([arXiv 2203.04910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.04910), [repo](https://github.com/ZaidQureshi/bam)). It requires the NVMe device to be unbound from the kernel driver and driven from GPU-resident queues. Your table drive carries a live ext4 filesystem, so this is structurally unavailable without dedicating a drive.
- **SCADA** is BaM productized, announced at FMS 2026, with cuFile and its full stack being open-sourced under the XIO-SIG GitHub organization (founding maintainers Google, Intel, Meta, NVIDIA). The platform named is Vera BlueField-4 STX ([StorageReview, Aug 2026](https://www.storagereview.com/news/nvidia-scada-puts-storage-control-on-the-gpu-as-cufile-goes-open-source)). Worth watching; not deployable on sm_86 with a stock driver today.
- **P2PDMA**: userspace NVMe P2P landed in **6.2**, but only for devices with a Controller Memory Buffer as source or destination ([kernel P2PDMA docs](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/v6.8/driver-api/pci/p2pdma.html), [LWN 931668](https://lwn.net/Articles/931668/)). Substantial refactoring for VFIO/dma-buf landed in **6.19** ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Linux-6.19-DMA-BUF-VFIO-PCI)). A consumer Phison drive is unlikely to expose a usable CMB. Not a path on 6.8.

### 3.5 LLM and recommender systems that serve tables from flash

**DeepSeek's own Engram paper is the most relevant source and the spec did not list it.** "Conditional Memory via Scalable Lookup", [arXiv 2601.07372](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07372v1), January 2026, [code](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/Engram). Three things it says that bear directly on your design:

- At inference "Engram tables are offloaded to host memory", and "offloading a 100B-parameter table incurs a negligible throughput penalty, peaking at only **2.8 %**". Their baseline is DRAM offload, not NVMe.
- The architecture "asynchronously prefetch[es] and transfers embeddings, overlapping communication with the on-device computation" — the same one-step-lead-time property you identified.
- It explicitly proposes what is rank 2 here: a "Multi-Level Cache Hierarchy: frequently accessed embeddings can be cached in faster storage tiers (e.g. GPU HBM or Host DRAM), while the long tail of rare patterns resides in slower media (e.g. NVMe SSD)", motivated by the Zipfian distribution of n-grams.

**Engram-Nine**, [arXiv 2601.16531v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16531v2), January 2026, measured the skew you would be exploiting: "top-100K 2-grams cover approximately **55 %** of training queries, and 3-grams cover approximately **23 %**" — on *their* training corpus, which is not your serving distribution. It also reports a useful negative for §6: replacing multi-head hashing with a minimal perfect hash for the hot tier gave essentially no quality benefit (validation loss 4.4799 vs 4.4809, within a std of 0.008–0.012) and cost 11–12 % throughput (~1693 vs ~1910 tok/s). **Do not re-lay-out the table to remove collisions for quality reasons.**

**Apple, "LLM in a flash"** ([arXiv 2312.11514](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.11514), ACL 2024): 4–5× on CPU and 20–25× on GPU over naive loading, for models twice the size of DRAM. Two transferable findings. First, "throughputs appropriate for sparse LLM inference are achievable on modern hardware using **32 KiB or larger random reads across multiple threads**" — small reads lose to latency-to-first-byte, which is the argument for co-locating rows. Second, and important, their co-location attempt **failed**: "bundling neurons with their highest coactivated neuron led to multiple loadings of highly active neurons, counteracting our goal… very active neurons are the closest friends of many others. We present this negative result to inspire future research." Their successful bundling was structural (a column of the up-projection with the matching row of the down-projection), not statistical.

**FlashEmbedding** (APSys'21, [PDF](https://www.cs.cityu.edu.hk/~huwan2/files/apsys21_wan.pdf)) is the closest analogue to your access pattern: embedding tables on SSD, random row lookups, up to **17.44× lower embedding-lookup latency** and 2.89× lower end-to-end latency, with an embedding-oriented software cache where **"cache size set to 1 % of embedding table size [is] sufficient for most cases"**. 1 % of your 195 GiB is 2.0 GB. **RecSSD** reduces end-to-end inference latency 2× via near-data processing; **RecFlash** ([arXiv 2604.25338](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25338)) reports up to 70.0 %/80.1 %/61.5 % latency reduction for RMC1/2/3 on Criteo TB with frequency-based data mapping. The recommender literature has been solving exactly your problem for five years and its consistent answer is *a small frequency-based cache*, not a faster syscall.

**DeepSeek 3FS** ([repo](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/3FS), [Fire-Flyer AI-HPC, arXiv 2408.14158](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.14158)) is instructive for its priorities rather than its code: it prioritizes random read throughput "above all else, and almost completely ignoring read caching", reaching 8 TB/s aggregate read and 40 GiB/s peak for its KVCache client. Its client API (USRBIO) is a user-space ring — the same architectural conclusion as rank 3. **PowerInfer-2** ([arXiv 2406.06282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06282)) uses segmented neuron caching and cluster-level pipelining precisely because "cold neurons require random I/O operations due to their sparse activation patterns, resulting in significantly reduced bandwidth". **DeepNVMe** ([PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/deepnvme-affordable-i-o-scaling-for-deep-learning-applications/), [2025 update](https://github.com/deepspeedai/DeepSpeed/blob/master/blogs/deepnvme/06-2025/README.md)) is engineered for near-peak *sequential* bandwidth via aggressive parallelization and pinned memory; its libaio/io_uring backend is reusable in spirit but its target workload is not yours.

### 3.6 Layout and algorithmic levers — checked against your own repository

I read `crates/engram/src/lib.rs` and `docs/plan.md` (read-only). Three facts there change what the literature implies:

- The 24 buckets per site are **disjoint prime-sized intervals whose sum exactly equals that site's row count**, each ~16 M rows ≈ 4.35 GB. So the spec's "uniformly scattered over the whole 195 GiB" is more precisely: **one read into each of 48 known, disjoint 4.35 GB regions**. There is no locality, but there *is* per-bucket structure, and that is what makes a per-bucket caching or placement policy possible.
- `token_map` maps 129,280 → **99,092**. This bounds the key space of the low-order n-grams.
- The hash constants live in GGUF metadata and the derivation is not yet in the engine; `SeededRows` stands in for it.

The consequence, marked **conditional and an estimate**: *if* one of the three n-gram orders is order 1 — consistent with "maximum N-gram size 3" and 24 = 3 × 8, but **not proven**, and you should read the GGUF hash constants before believing it — then that order's distinct key space is at most 99,092 regardless of its bucket holding 16 M rows. Its 8 buckets can therefore only ever touch 8 × 99,092 rows = **216 MB**, which is permanently cacheable and would remove **16 of 48 reads deterministically, forever**. Adding the top-100K 2-grams for the second order costs another 218 MB and, at Engram-Nine's 55 % coverage, removes roughly 9 more. That is 48 → ~23 reads per token for under 500 MB of DRAM. Even at half that hit rate it dominates every kernel-interface lever in this report.

On re-laying out the table so one token's reads cluster: the literature says don't. Engram-Nine shows collision-freedom is not worth 11 % throughput; Apple shows statistical co-location backfires. The one re-layout with a large payoff is a **model-side** change, not a storage change: the 24 rows of a site concatenate into one 6,144-wide vector (24 × 256 values), so if a site used a single hash selecting one contiguous 6,528 B record instead of 24 independent hashes, you would issue **2 reads per token instead of 48**. That is a training-time decision, and Engram-Nine's MPHF hot tier is the nearest thing in the literature. Worth naming in the design discussion even though you cannot adopt it for this checkpoint.

On a second drive: the 980 PRO's 2 TB comfortably holds a 195 GiB replica. Haas & Leis Fig. 2a shows random-read IOPS scaling linearly with drive count (12.5 M IOPS across 8 PM1733s, 1.56 M each). Halving per-drive queue depth mostly buys p99, which is your weakest column.

- **The drive.** `PSEIN004TA87MC0` is a **Phison-controller 4 TB OEM M.2 Gen4 x4 TLC drive** (HP part M93208-001), rated 7,400–7,600 MB/s sequential read ([ITCreations listing](https://www.itcreations.com/product/149847), [PassMark entry](https://www.harddrivebenchmark.net/hdd.php?hdd=PSEIN004TA87MC0&id=33950)). Whether it has an onboard DRAM L2P cache or relies on a Host Memory Buffer I could not determine — see §5, and it matters for your tail.
- **QD1 4 KB latency, 2025–2026.** Most Gen5 enterprise drives sit at **60–90 µs**, with the Kioxia CD9P-R an outlier near **30 µs**; the CM7-R measured 78 µs at 714,623 IOPS in VDBench 4K random read ([StorageReview](https://www.storagereview.com/review/kioxia-cd9p-r-review-read-intensive-gen5-up-to-61-44tb), [CM7-R review](https://www.storagereview.com/review/kioxia-cm7-r-e3-s-ssd-review)). Your measured 92 µs per major fault is entirely consistent with a Gen4 consumer drive plus fault overhead — **the device is behaving normally**. Note that a sub-100 µs *wall* target per token is at or below the QD1 device floor; it is only reachable because you overlap, which is the argument for rank 1, not for a faster device path.
- **NVMe KV command set** would let you address a value by key instead of by LBA, removing the host-side translation table ([NVM Express spec](https://nvmexpress.org/specification/key-value-command-set-specification/)). No KV SSD is present and none is commercially easy to buy. **FDP** and **ZNS** target write amplification and endurance; your table is read-only. **Read Recovery Level** trades error-recovery time for tail latency and is a per-namespace feature few consumer drives implement. **Optane** was discontinued in 2022; Haas & Leis note it has "very low latencies with very small page sizes" and would have been the ideal medium for this exact workload, but "alternative storage technologies such as Optane did not achieve commercial success and were consequently discontinued". **CXL** is not available: the Threadripper 5975WX is a PCIe 4.0 sWRX8 part with no CXL support.

### 3.8 Measurement pitfalls

- **`posix_fadvise(DONTNEED)` rounds inward.** "Requests to discard partial pages are ignored"; the offset and length must be page-aligned or the edges survive ([posix_fadvise(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/posix_fadvise.2.html)). You already hit this. There is a second, opposite historical bug worth knowing — [a patch titled "mm: Do not discard partial pages with POSIX_FADV_DONTNEED"](https://lkml.rescloud.iu.edu/1604.1/06768.html) exists because the last partial page *was* being discarded — so the behaviour differed across kernels. Verify on 6.8 rather than trusting either man page or memory.
- **`/proc/self/io` `read_bytes` vs `majflt` measure different things.** `read_bytes` counts bytes actually fetched through the block layer, so it captures amplification; `majflt` counts faults that caused I/O. Your two agree at ~1 page per row (208,660 B ÷ 4096 = 50.9 pages, matching 50.9 major faults), which is the clean signature of `MADV_RANDOM` working. **Under `O_DIRECT` both instruments go blind**: there are no faults at all and no page-cache accounting. Before building rank 3, plan the replacement instrumentation — completion counts from the ring, plus `/sys/block/nvme0n1/stat` deltas around the lease window.
- **Separate caller CPU from wall.** This is the largest measurement gap and it is discussed in §6.
- **The 4 KB page is the right unit to compare against.** Haas & Leis: 4 KB "allows for the highest random-read performance and lowest latency", only 8 % below the maximum bandwidth achievable with large pages, and cuts amplification 4× versus 16 KB. Your instinct to go smaller is right in direction and worth about 25 µs of hidden time.
- **Drive-count and queue-depth scaling need ~100 outstanding I/Os per device** to reach rated IOPS (Haas & Leis Fig. 4, ~1000 across 8 drives). At QD48 on one drive you are well inside the linear region, which is why your device wait disappears — do not read that as "the device has headroom for free".

## 4. Contradictions between sources

1. **Sub-4K reads: harmful vs fine.** Haas & Leis measured 512 B reads as *worse* in both IOPS and latency than 4 KB on Samsung PM1733 datacenter drives, attributing it to FTL mapping granularity. Micron/NVIDIA demonstrated 230 M 512 B random-read IOPS at ~95 % of rated IOPS on Gen6 9650 drives. Both are credible; they are three years and two controller generations apart. Unresolved for your Phison Gen4 drive — it is a two-line fio arm to settle locally.
2. **io_uring CPU cost per I/O: ~2.6 µs vs ~0.4 µs.** Haas & Leis derive ~6,500 cycles for submit+reap with the filesystem and page cache disabled; arXiv 2512.04859 claims batching cuts cycles per operation 5–6×, but its own Table 1 shows the batched transaction costing *more* cycles than the single one. I could not reconcile these and report the range.
3. **Collision-free hot tiers: proposed vs refuted.** Engram proposes a multi-level cache exploiting Zipf as future work; Engram-Nine implemented the collision-free hot tier and found no quality gain and an 11–12 % throughput loss. These are not actually in conflict — Engram's motivation is *I/O*, Nine's test was *quality* — but they are easy to read as agreeing when they do not. The caching case survives; the collision-freedom case does not.
4. **Batching always helps.** The DBMS paper's own Table 2 shows SSD write latency rising with batch size (11.51 µs at batch 1, 24.22 at 8, 200.85 at 128). Batching trades latency for throughput. For 48 ops once per 10–40 ms you are firmly in the good region, but the sign is not universal.

## 5. What I could not determine

- **Whether this Phison drive is DRAM-equipped or HMB-based.** This matters more than anything else in §7: with a host memory buffer, uniformly random reads over 4 TB miss the L2P mapping cache on essentially every access and pay a second flash lookup — a device-wait term that QD48 hides in p50 and exposes in p99. The lead can settle it on the box with `nvme id-ctrl /dev/nvme0` and reading the `hmpre`/`hmmin` fields (non-zero means the drive requests a host memory buffer), plus `nvme id-ctrl | grep -i vwc` and the vendor's `Phison` SMART page.
- **Whether the three n-gram orders include order 1.** The GGUF metadata carries the hash constants; nothing in `crates/engram` reads them yet. The rank-2 sizing in §3.6 hinges on this.
- **Whether `IORING_OP_READ` multishot exists for regular files on 6.8.** Multishot is well documented for accept/recv; I found no evidence either way for buffered or direct file reads, and did not want to assert it.
- **The exact 512 B vs 4 KB latency of this drive.** No review of this OEM part measures QD1 latency at sub-4K block sizes.
- **Whether the ext4 extent lookup per `O_DIRECT` request is material at 48 reads/token.** The files are large and presumably lightly fragmented, so extents should be few and cached, but I have no number.
- **Numbers from Phylliida's `v41-flash` port.** `docs/plan.md` records 6–8 tok/s on one 3090 with expert cache plus engram SSD serving, and also records that the figure "is not anywhere in the tree". It is the only end-to-end datapoint for engram-from-SSD that exists, and it is unverified.

## 6. Improvement spots outside this spec (report only — I changed nothing)

1. **The measurement conflates wall latency with caller CPU, and `AGENTS.md` says the latter is what a step costs.** Every arm in the table is a wall number measured on one thread. Given a full step of lead time and 32 cores, the question the engine cares about is "how many microseconds does the prefetch steal from the thread the step waits on". Arm 3 (prefetch for t+1 while reading t) gained only 25 µs over arm 2, which strongly suggests the prefetch ran on the calling thread. Adding a **helper-thread arm** would likely be the largest single improvement in the table and needs no new interface, no dependency, and no kernel change. It should be measured before io_uring is built, because it changes what io_uring is *for*: with a helper thread, io_uring's value is the helper's CPU efficiency and page-cache avoidance, not latency.
2. **The consumer side is not free even with a perfect prefetch.** If the step thread reads rows through the mapping it still takes 48 minor faults (~90 µs) regardless of how they were prefetched. If the helper copies rows into a private 13 KiB buffer, the step thread pays one ~1 µs memcpy. `crates/engram`'s current design borrows rows from the mapping and "nothing is copied" — a deliberate choice that is right for the mmap path and wrong for every path in this report.
3. **Uniform random ids are the pessimal workload, and the design note says caching is not the design.** `crates/engram/src/lib.rs` states "caching it is not the design: each site is mapped and read where it lies." Both the Engram paper and twenty years of recommender-systems practice say the opposite. Before committing to that, instrument distinct-row reuse on a real token stream: if the hit rate resembles Zipf at all, a few hundred MB of DRAM beats every kernel technique in this report combined.
4. **"Uniformly scattered over the whole 195 GiB" understates what you know.** The 24 buckets are disjoint intervals with known boundaries, each ~4.35 GB. That is not locality, but it is the structure a per-bucket policy (cache the low-order buckets, replicate the high-order ones to the second drive) would be built on.
5. **The amplification figure is doing more rhetorical work than it deserves.** 16× sounds alarming, but at 192 KiB per token it is ~27 µs of device transfer on a 7.4 GB/s link, and the device wait is already hidden. `docs/plan.md` names "확대 16 → 2–4배" as one of the two next levers; on the arithmetic it is the *smaller* of the two by an order of magnitude. The reason to adopt `O_DIRECT` is the 181 µs of page-cache machinery and the 90 µs of faults, not the bytes.
6. **`/proc/self/io` and `majflt` will stop working the moment you adopt `O_DIRECT`.** The instrumentation that makes `engram-rate` trustworthy is specific to the buffered path. Plan its replacement in the same round, or the first io_uring arm will be a number with no witness.
7. **Page-cache budget is a shared resource nobody is accounting for.** With ~249 GiB of 256 GB in weights, the page cache available for engram is small and every `WILLNEED` competes with it. `O_DIRECT` is the only option in this report that returns that budget, and a DRAM hot-row cache spends it explicitly rather than implicitly. That trade deserves to be a line in the design, not an emergent property of reclaim.
