The harbor lights came on one by one as the ferry eased into the dock, and the crowd on the pier leaned forward to see who was coming home. The season had turned early that year; the first cold nights arrived before the end of September, and by the middle of October the hills above the town were already bare. Every evening the same routine: the last ferry from the mainland, the mooring lines thrown and pulled taut, the ramp lowered with a groan, and then the slow shuffle of passengers carrying bags and boxes down onto the pier. Some of them came every week, and the men at the fish market knew their names. Others appeared once and were never seen again, and nobody asked why. The town lived on what the sea gave and what the ferry brought, and it asked for little else. There had been years of plenty, when the herring came close to the shore in silver schools so dense that the water seemed to boil, and there had been lean years when the boats came back light and the merchants stood at the quay with their hands in their pockets, saying nothing. Through all of it the lighthouse kept its watch, sweeping the water every twelve seconds, and the keeper climbed the hundred and forty steps each afternoon to clean the lens and wind the mechanism. He was the third of his family to hold the post. His grandmother had kept the light through the war, when the channel was mined and the convoy escorts gathered off the point before running south in the dark. She wrote her log in a small gray notebook that still sat in the drawer beneath the radio room desk, the pages soft at the edges, the pencil faded to brown. The entries were terse: wind, visibility, the number of ships, the times the lamp was lit and extinguished. Once, between a weather report and a note about supplies, she had written a single line about a moonrise so clear that the whole channel lay silver from the point to the far headland, and the keeper had read that line a hundred times without learning anything more about her than the entries themselves told. The town had changed around the harbor more slowly than the cities had changed around their rivers. The canning plant closed the year the treaties changed the fishing grounds, and half the fleet was sold south within a decade. The boats that remained were smaller, and their crews worked the close grounds in short trips, coming home the same night. Children still learned the water the way children in other towns learned a field behind the school: as a place with rules, and weather, and work. On the pier the crowd thinned as the passengers found their rides or set out on foot up the hill. An old man with a canvas bag stopped at the bottom of the ramp, looked back at the ferry, and then turned and walked slowly toward the fish market, where the lights were still on and the day's catch was already iced in the long bins. Behind him the crew made the vessel fast for the night, and the engineer shut down the engines one at a time, and the water went quiet against the pilings. The last of the gulls circled the mast twice and drifted off toward the breakwater. In the morning the fog might be back, and the ferry would sound its horn at the point, and the keeper would already be at the top of the tower, wiping the salt from the glass with a chamois cloth, getting ready for another day of the same careful work that had kept the light burning through every season his family could remember, and the town below would wake to the smell of the sea and the sound of the first truck on the coastal road, carrying the catch north while it was still
 fresh enough to fetch a fair price. The merchant at the first stall had kept a ledger since before the plant closed, and he could tell from the weight of a crate how the grounds had fared that week, without asking. His daughter ran the second stall now, and she counted the notes with the same quick rhythm he had, tucking them into the same canvas apron with the same crease down the middle. Between the stalls and the ferry ramp the same three benches had stood for as long as anyone could remember, and the same arguments gathered on them each afternoon: the weather, the quotas, the price of diesel, the ambitions of the council, and the eternal question of whether the breakwater needed the repairs the engineers kept promising.
